Scratch-Built Mid-Power Sounding Rocket

FIRST TEST FLIGHT COMPLETE • ROCKET RECOVERED INTACT
01 Design → 02 Simulation → 03 Fabrication → 04 Avionics Bench → 05 Recovery → 06 Flight Testing

After flying commercial Estes model rocket kits and running into practical issues like burned wadding and wind drift, I designed, simulated, and built a 36.4" scratch-built model rocket from the ground up. I modeled the airframe and internal components in SolidWorks, ran flight simulations in OpenRocket to set a 1.60 cal stability margin, and 3D-printed custom couplers and the nose cone on my Bambu Lab A1. On the scale, the empty airframe weighed in at 309g (modeled at 326g with estimated avionics weight). On September 19, 2026, I completed the maiden test flight on an Estes D12-3 motor at Freestone Park, launching with an empty payload bay to focus the test on basic vehicle flight performance. The rocket completed a clean flight, deployed its parachute near apogee, and was recovered intact on the grass with no visible structural damage.

01 Preliminary Flights, Stability & Airframe Build

I started by flying commercial Estes kits to see how model rockets handle on the field. The problems I encountered—burned wadding and excessive drift—directly shaped how I designed the recovery system, selected fin geometry in OpenRocket, and built the airframe.

Subscale Flights • Lessons Learned
Commercial Kit Flights & Failure Modes
I flew commercial Estes kits to see pad dynamics and recovery firsthand. Hot motor gases burned right through standard recovery wadding, and desert breezes easily carried the rockets away. That showed me I needed a positive Nomex piston and realistic drift estimates.
Inspect Tracking Footage →
OpenRocket Simulation
Vehicle Stability & Mass Balance
I modeled the full vehicle in OpenRocket before building. With the Center of Pressure at 28.01" and the Center of Gravity at 23.86", the simulated static stability margin came out to 1.60 calibers, ensuring passive stability on both D12-3 and F32T-6 motors.
Airframe Assembly • Flown Vehicle
Assembled 36.4" Sounding Rocket
The completed airframe integrates BT-80 body tubes, a 3D-printed ogive nose cone, a central payload coupler bay, basswood fins, and dual launch lugs. The empty airframe weighed 309g on the scale and was painted in gold livery before launch.

Physical caliper measurements of the BT-80 airframe tubes revealed an inner diameter of 65.5 mm (1.0 mm larger than catalog spec). Avionics Telemetry Bring-Up

RP2040 MicroPython
Inspect Telemetry Bench

I started building the avionics on a breadboard using a Raspberry Pi Pico running MicroPython. I tested clocking and serial bus communication with an initial DHT sensor loop, and wired up an MPU-6500 6-axis IMU to verify sensor polling. This bench setup proved the microcontroller logic ahead of flight. The rocket flew its first test flight with an empty payload bay, and the next step is adding a BMP390 precision barometer and MicroSD logger to record altitude and acceleration on Flight 2.

Flight Computer:Raspberry Pi Pico (RP2040)
Bench Testing:MicroPython • DHT • MPU-6500
Flight 2 Sensors:BMP390 Baro + MPU-6500 IMU

Recovery System & Kevlar Harness

Recovery Mechanics
Inspect Recovery Rig

Engineered to eliminate wadding burn-through experienced on earlier flights, incorporating a flame-resistant Nomex cloth piston designed to isolate recovery gear from hot ejection gases. Replaced elastic cords with 9 feet of 350-lb braided Kevlar shock cord anchored directly to the motor mount and payload coupler arch with high-temperature JB-Weld epoxy. Sized a 24" ripstop nylon parachute for a controlled 5.65 m/s touchdown velocity.

Shock Tether:9-ft 350 lb Braided Kevlar
Thermal Shield:Nomex Isolation Piston
Touchdown Velocity:5.65 m/s (24" Parachute)

Propulsion & Positive Retention

Mechanical Retention
Inspect Motor Retention

Replaced fragile plastic Z-clips with dual #6 steel screws and 3/8" zinc fender washers that overlap the motor casing rim. Pre-threaded pilot holes were treated with thin cyanoacrylate (CA) glue to harden plastic thread engagement. A 3D-printed D12 stopper ring absorbs forward thrust forces.

Retention Rig:Dual #6 screws + 3/8" washers
Thread Hardening:Thin CA glue thread infusion
Motor Interface:24mm (D12-3 / F32T-6)

Payload Carrier CAD

SolidWorks Modeling
Inspect SolidWorks Part

Modeled the central airframe payload carrier in SolidWorks with integrated coupler shoulders, internal mounting bosses for a pull-ribbon avionics sled, and a heavy-duty 4-legged shock cord anchor arch spanning the aft bulkhead to evenly distribute parachute opening shock.

CAD Platform:SolidWorks 3D CAD

03 First Test Flight & Simulation Comparison

After several months of designing and fabricating the rocket, I flew it for the first time on September 19, 2026, at Freestone Park in Gilbert, AZ. Launch conditions were approximately 100°F with ~7 mph wind from the WSW. The rocket flew on an Estes D12-3 motor with the 5-ft launch rod tilted 5° into the wind, and launched with an empty payload bay to focus the test on basic vehicle flight performance. The rocket had a clean liftoff from the rod, a stable ascent, deployed its parachute near apogee, and drifted back down onto the grass.

Testing Notes & Instrumentation Limitations

Tripod Camera Overheating: The dedicated tripod camera overheated in the ~100°F Arizona heat and failed to record, so the available video for analysis came from handheld phone footage.

Velocity Estimate Excluded: Video analysis produced an estimated velocity of 6.025 m/s shortly after leaving the launch rod, compared with 13.8 m/s predicted by OpenRocket. However, the video analysis was not reliable enough to determine whether this difference reflects the actual flight, so I excluded the velocity estimate from the main simulation comparison.

Next Steps: Flight 1 confirmed that the airframe geometry, 1.60 cal stability margin, motor retention, and Nomex parachute deployment work as expected in flight. The next milestone is completing the flight computer—wiring the BMP390 barometer and MPU-6500 IMU to the Raspberry Pi Pico, building the payload bay mounting sled, and recording onboard altitude and acceleration on Flight 2.
